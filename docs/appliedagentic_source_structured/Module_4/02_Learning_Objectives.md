# What You Will Be Able to Do by the End of This Module

Module 4 focuses on one of the most consequential aspects of leading with AI: **understanding and managing the security risks it introduces**. By the end of this module, you will have the practical knowledge to diagnose threats, evaluate AI systems critically, and advocate for the safeguards your organization needs.

Here are the three core learning objectives, with context on why each one matters:

## Learning Objective 1: Diagnose Common AI-Driven Cybersecurity Threats

> 💡 **What This Means**
> "Diagnosing" a threat is different from simply knowing it exists. It means being able to recognize how a threat *shows up in practice* — in your organization's specific context, in real business scenarios, and often in ways that do not look threatening at first glance.

Modern cyberattacks — especially those augmented by AI — are increasingly sophisticated. They are designed to blend in with normal business activity, exploit trust relationships, and bypass the kind of checks that worked in the pre-AI era.

This learning objective is about developing the pattern recognition and critical judgment to spot these threats before — or as soon as — they occur.

**Threats you will be able to diagnose:**

| Threat Type | What It Looks Like | Why It Is Dangerous |
|---|---|---|
| **Phishing and spear-phishing** | A convincing email from a known contact, with a legitimate-looking attachment | One click can install malware and give attackers access to your entire network |
| **Deepfakes** | A video or audio clip that appears to show a real person saying or doing something they never did | Can manipulate financial decisions, spread disinformation, or damage reputations |
| **AI impersonation** | A voice clone or chatbot that convincingly poses as an executive, vendor, or employee | Employees may transfer funds, share credentials, or take harmful actions based on a fabricated identity |
| **Prompt injection** | Hidden or malicious instructions embedded in text given to an AI system | Can trick the AI into leaking sensitive data, bypassing safety controls, or taking unauthorized actions |

**What you will be able to do:**
- Identify the characteristics of each major AI-driven threat
- Map real or hypothetical attack scenarios to the relevant threat category
- Recognize the conditions that make an organization particularly vulnerable
- Describe these threats clearly to non-technical colleagues and leadership

# Learning Objective 2: Implement Foundational Defense Strategies

> 💡 **What This Means**
> Knowing about a threat is not enough. This objective is about being able to take action — implementing practical, proven strategies that reduce risk both before an attack happens (prevention) and during or after one (response).

This module introduces two major frameworks that structure cybersecurity thinking:

1. **The Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain** — a model that breaks any attack into seven distinct phases, helping you see exactly where interventions are possible and where your organization is most exposed.

2. **The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** — a flexible, widely adopted model that organizes cybersecurity into five core functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover.

> ⚠️ **Why This Matters**
> Frameworks matter because they give everyone in an organization — from the IT team to the C-suite — a shared language and a shared structure for thinking about security. Without a framework, security efforts tend to be reactive, ad hoc, and inconsistent. With one, they become systematic, proactive, and measurable.

**What you will be able to do:**
- Explain the seven phases of the Cyber Kill Chain and identify defensive opportunities at each phase
- Describe the five functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework and apply them to AI-specific risks
- Translate each framework element into concrete, actionable steps your organization can take
- Ask the right questions of your security, IT, and compliance teams to assess your current posture

## Learning Objective 3: Critique the Limitations of AI Perception and Reasoning

> 💡 **What This Means**
> This is perhaps the most nuanced — and most underappreciated — of the three objectives. It is not about external attackers. It is about the risks that come from the AI systems themselves: what they get wrong, why they get it wrong, and what the consequences can be.

AI systems are powerful. They are also, in important ways, *unreliable*. Understanding those limitations is essential for anyone deploying or overseeing AI in a business context.

The two main failure modes covered in this objective are:

**Hallucination**: When an AI generates information that is plausible-sounding but factually incorrect — and presents it with confident fluency. A well-known LLM does not "know" when it is wrong. It generates responses based on patterns in its training data, not verified facts. In a medical, legal, or financial context, a confident wrong answer can be deeply harmful.

**Bias**: AI systems learn from human-generated data — and human data reflects human biases. Without deliberate effort to identify and correct for these biases, AI systems will perpetuate and often amplify them. Real-world examples include AI hiring tools that discriminated against women and older applicants, and tenant-screening algorithms that systematically disadvantaged Black and Hispanic applicants.

**What you will be able to do:**
- Explain what AI hallucination is and why it occurs
- Identify the types of high-stakes decisions where hallucination risk is most serious
- Describe how bias enters AI systems and how it can be mitigated
- Evaluate an AI system's fitness for a given use case, including its potential failure modes
- Advocate for appropriate oversight mechanisms and safeguards in your organization's AI deployments

# How These Three Objectives Fit Together

These three learning objectives are not independent. They form a coherent picture of what it means to lead securely in an AI-powered organization:

```
External Threats     →     Defense Frameworks     →     Internal AI Risks
(What attacks us)         (How we respond)              (What can go wrong inside)
       ↑                          ↑                               ↑
  Objective 1               Objective 2                     Objective 3
```

Understanding external threats helps you build the right defenses. Understanding your defense frameworks helps you respond to those threats effectively. And understanding AI's internal limitations helps you make sure that the AI systems you deploy to help you do not become threats in themselves.

# Key Takeaways

1. **Diagnosing AI-driven threats requires pattern recognition**, not just awareness — knowing what phishing, deepfakes, impersonation, and prompt injection look like in real business scenarios.
2. **Defense frameworks provide structure** — the Cyber Kill Chain and the NIST CSF give organizations and leaders a shared language for thinking about, measuring, and improving security.
3. **AI's internal limitations are a strategic risk**, not just a technical nuisance — hallucinations and bias can cause serious harm in regulated industries and high-stakes decisions.
4. **This module is designed for business leaders**, not technical specialists — you will leave with the knowledge to ask the right questions, make informed decisions, and advocate for the security investments your organization needs.
5. **Security is not a one-time project** — it is an ongoing practice. These objectives equip you to build that practice into your organization's culture and operations.
